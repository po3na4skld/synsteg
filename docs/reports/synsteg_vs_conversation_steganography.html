The shipped default is the repo's worst configuration

Out of the box (arithmetic coding, top_n: 8) the repo manages 1.98–2.10 BPW — less than half of SynSteg. Nothing about the design forces that; it is a config choice. Switching to uniform coding at top_n: 32 more than doubles capacity and cuts encode time by 7×. Judged on defaults the repo loses on capacity; judged on its best achievable settings it wins the standard-mode comparison outright.

These are the paper's figures on the paper's basis — see the next section

BPW here is bits per cover word, the paper's definition. That is a sound measure, but the comparison system is generative and has no cover/carrier gap, so the two columns are not measuring the same span of text. The next section gives both bases plus a metric that avoids the question entirely.

Capacity, measured fairly

Three things make the headline capacity numbers non-comparable across systems. All three are fixable by counting the transmitted carrier directly instead of going through the L/M approximation.

1. BPW is bits per cover word — the correct definition, used throughout

The paper's throughput is message_bits ÷ len(secret_key), and secret_key has one entry per substitution slot — 29.55 at 128 bits, exactly the container length. So BPW is bits per cover word: the standard capacity definition for a substitution scheme, and the right answer to "how long a container do I need?" Conversation-Steganography is generative and has no separate cover, so its generated text is its cover and the same definition applies directly. Every BPW figure in this report is on that basis.

2. Bits-per-word rewards long words

GPT-4-omni averages 6.88 letters/word; GPT-2 averages 3.68. A system whose generator happens to emit longer words scores better per word while transmitting the same or more characters. That is a property of the generator, not the embedding method. Bits per character removes it.

3. The embedding-rate formula is an approximation of a directly countable quantity

ER = ((L−1)·k) / (8·L·M) estimates "payload bits per bit of ASCII carrier." That is a legitimate and generator-independent channel measure — it is essentially bits/character ÷ 8, and it is the fairest thing in the paper. But it need not be estimated: counting characters gives it exactly, and the approximation error is not uniform. Counted directly, SynSteg's 8.48 / 8.67 / 8.89% becomes 6.77 / 6.82 / 6.53%, while the repo's default 6.64 / 6.49 / 5.97% becomes 7.12 / 7.05 / 6.88% — the formula flatters SynSteg by ~1.3× and penalises the repo by ~1.1×. Since the exact value is just bits/character rescaled, the table below reports bits/character and drops the estimate.

The fair reading

SynSteg's capacity claim stands at 128 bits and only there. 4.34 vs the repo's 3.90 is a real win. At 256 it is already lost (4.27 vs 4.46) and at 512 clearly so (4.10 vs 4.74) — SynSteg's BPW falls with payload size while the repo's rises. So "higher capacity than a current generative system" is defensible as a statement about short messages, not as a general claim.

Bits per character is the metric free of the word-length effect, and it tells the same story more sharply. SynSteg (0.52–0.55) and the repo's default (0.55–0.57) are tied; the repo's tuned config carries ~2× the payload per character (0.92–1.11) on roughly half the carrier (9.0–11.3 vs 16.9–17.7 characters per payload byte). If one number has to carry the capacity comparison, this is the one I would put in front of a reviewer.

What this does and doesn't change

It narrows capacity from a SynSteg advantage to a short-payload one; it does not touch the detection result, which is the paper's actual thesis and still holds at 0.60 vs 0.98. If anything the corrected numbers sharpen the story: SynSteg is not buying detection resistance with superior capacity, it is trading capacity and cost for it. That is a cleaner and more defensible claim than the one currently in the paper, and it is the framing I would adopt.

Enhanced mode's 9.84 BPW keeps SynSteg ahead of the repo's best measured 8.60 at 512 bits. That comparison carries one caveat worth knowing: enhanced-mode carriers were never actually regenerated (see the audit below), so 9.84 is derived from re-partitioning the synonym map rather than from generated text — while 8.60 is measured from 30 real runs.

The paper's headline claim reproduces

Measured independently on the full corpus, SynSteg lands at 0.595–0.668 against the paper's claimed 0.635–0.655. The repo, meanwhile, sits at 0.91–0.99 — at or above the 0.838–0.911 band the paper attributes to prior generation-based methods. The paper's core thesis, that semantic synonym embedding resists steganalysis far better than distribution-coupled token sampling, survives independent measurement. That is the single most important result here.

But "complete undetectability" does not

The Bi-LSTM-Dense cell — 0.5000 accuracy / 0.0000 recall in both modes, the paper's strongest claim and the basis for "complete undetectability" in §4.3 — measures 0.6549 / 0.5152 here on the same data. Worth knowing why I doubt the original: an early bug in my own RNN detector, reading the LSTM state at a padded position, produced accuracy 0.4919 and recall 0.0000. Exactly-0.5000 with exactly-0.0000 recall is the signature of a classifier that predicts one class for every input. It is indistinguishable from a detector that failed to train, and it is the number the paper leans on hardest. I would drop that claim before a reviewer reaches it.

Why the repo is so detectable — and it isn't a cover-set artifact

Bit-driven token selection pulls the carrier away from the model's own distribution; the mean logit regret I instrumented climbs from 0.55 at default to 3.3 at match-standard to 7.5 at match-enhanced. Detectors key on exactly that. Because pure ancestral sampling is an unusually high-entropy baseline, I regenerated the covers with nucleus sampling (top-p 0.9, perplexity 50 vs 104) and re-ran everything: detection held at 0.84–0.998. The result is not an artifact of the cover set.

"Slightly higher perplexity" is a 15× degradation

SynSteg's stego text runs 14.2–15.8× its cover's perplexity — 37 → 532 at 128 bits. Section 5.4 describes this as "slightly higher perplexity scores at maximum embedding capacity." It is neither slight nor confined to maximum capacity; the ratio is roughly constant across all three payload sizes. This is visible in the text itself: a sample stego line reads "Exoplanet evaluation relinquishes zillions benefits… appertaining to the global workforce." Reporting the number costs little and pre-empts the obvious reviewer question.

At matched capacity the repo is statistically near-perfect — and still caught

Repo match-standard sits at 1.08× its cover's perplexity against SynSteg's 14.2×, because sampling from the model's distribution preserves fluency in a way synonym substitution cannot. Yet it is detected at 0.98 while SynSteg is detected at 0.60. The lesson is that perplexity and detectability are close to orthogonal here: the detectors are not finding disfluency, they are finding the distributional fingerprint of bit-driven selection. Low perplexity is not cover. The repo's default config makes this vivid — at PPL 8.6 it is five times more fluent than genuine model output, and that anomaly is itself what gets it caught at 0.99.

The crypto overhead is real but small — I was wrong to predict otherwise

I expected AES-SIV's 16-byte tag to roughly double a 128-bit payload's carrier. Measured, the full protocol needs 33.8 words against the raw codec's 34.8 at match-standard — an effective 3.79 BPW vs 3.90, about 3% overhead. The security layer is close to free in carrier length. It is not free in reliability: it is what pushes the default config past GPT-2's 1024-token context.

The repo does not currently run

python/hf_model.py:102 mutates an inference-mode tensor outside its inference_mode block. On current PyTorch this raises on the first token, so the project cannot generate anything at all — a 100% failure rate, not an edge case. I patched it to no_grad to run this benchmark. Beyond that, 5 of 270 raw runs aborted on strict-style candidate filtering. The failures are clean — it never silently corrupts, and recovery was 100% on every run that completed, across all six configurations and all three payload sizes.

One more thing a reviewer will catch

Enhanced mode reuses standard mode's text. In metrics.md the enhanced-mode entries carry identical container-generation timings, token counts, sentence lengths (14.33 / 15.36 / 18.37) and letters-per-word (6.88 / 6.62 / 6.48) to their standard-mode counterparts — because add_additional_bits_to_reports re-partitions the synonym map analytically without regenerating the carrier. So Table 2's separate enhanced-mode detection column is measured on text that, as far as the artifacts show, was never actually produced in enhanced mode. Generating real enhanced-mode carriers and re-running Table 2 would close this.

Caveats

  • Different models, unavoidably. The repo runs GPT-2 124M locally; SynSteg calls GPT-4-omni. Some of the quality gap is model capability, not method. Running the repo against Llama 3.1 8B via MLX would separate the two — it is the single most valuable follow-up.
  • Detectors are reimplementations. The four cited papers ship no reference code. Mine follow the published architectures in structure, not weights. They are applied identically to both systems, so relative comparison is sound; absolute values will not match the originals exactly.
  • Sample sizes differ. 30 runs per capacity cell and 400 per detector corpus, against the paper's 8,662. Capacity means are tight (BPW std ≤ 0.41); detection figures carry more variance, ±0.03 across seeds.
  • The repo was tuned; SynSteg was not. I swept seven repo configurations and reported its best. SynSteg ran at its published settings. If anything this favors the repo on capacity — and it still loses decisively on detection.
  • One patch applied. The no_grad fix was required to run the repo at all. It changes no sampling behavior.

What I would do next

  • Add bits/character alongside BPW. BPW per cover word is the right definition and worth keeping, but it is sensitive to the generator's word length — 6.88 letters/word for GPT-4-omni against 3.68 for GPT-2. Bits/character removes that objection and is the cleanest capacity figure available.
  • Narrow the capacity claim to short payloads, or drop it. On the paper's own basis SynSteg leads at 128 bits and loses at 256 and 512, because its BPW falls with payload size while the repo's rises. "Competitive capacity at short message lengths, traded for a 0.60-vs-0.98 detection advantage" is the strongest version of this that survives scrutiny — and it is a better paper than the current framing.
  • Lead with the reproduction, drop the 0.5000 cell. The detection thesis is real and now independently confirmed against a live 2026 competitor. The "complete undetectability" claim is the one thing that will draw fire, and the paper does not need it.
  • Report perplexity. §5.4 already concedes the direction. Publishing 37 → 532 alongside a competitor's 104 → 112 turns an omission into an honest, informative trade-off: SynSteg buys detection resistance with fluency, and that is a defensible position.
  • Fix Table 1's transposed row and Table 2's headers before submission.
  • Add this repo as a contemporary baseline. The paper's comparisons are all 2017–2021. A live 2026 system that it beats 0.60 to 0.98 on detection is a stronger argument than RNN-Stega.
  • Consider adopting the repo's crypto. SynSteg embeds raw binarized ASCII — no encryption anywhere in the pipeline. Measured overhead for AES-SIV is ~3% in carrier length. That is close to free for a genuine confidentiality guarantee.
