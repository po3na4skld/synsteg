Can SynSteg remove a watermark?

Anthropic began watermarking Claude's text output in August 2026 using a variant of SynthID‑Text. Because SynSteg rewrites words in place, it is fair to ask whether embedding a payload also strips the watermark. It does — but only by damaging the text past the point of usefulness, and its advantage over swapping words at random is real yet negligible.

Verdict

SynSteg is not a watermark-removal method.

Watermark destruction is driven overwhelmingly by how many words change, not by SynSteg's payload-driven choice of which synonym to use. A random-substitution control at a matched edit rate removes nearly as much signal while carrying no payload at all. Every configuration that meaningfully suppresses the detector also multiplies perplexity by ten to fourteen, and every configuration that preserves readability leaves the watermark perfectly detectable.

Best removal (SynSteg b=1) 4.7% of watermark signal left — at 10.5× perplexity and a 28.9% edit rate.
SynSteg's edge over random 8.8% of baseline signal at b=1. Statistically real, practically irrelevant.
Readable attack (1.5× ppl) AUC 1.000 Watermark survives intact, TPR at 1% FPR still 1.000.

How this was measured

Anthropic keeps its watermark key and detector private, so real Claude output cannot be scored. This study substitutes an open SynthID-Text instance — the same algorithm family Anthropic names — which makes the question measurable end to end.

  1. Score. The training-free mean g-value detector from the SynthID-Text paper. Watermarked text sits above the 0.5 null; an attack that works drags it back down. N-grams whose context already appeared are masked out, mirroring the reference detector, because the watermark deliberately does not re-bias repeated contexts.
  2. Attack. SynSteg's substitution mechanism — walk the text, and where a word has at least 2b synonyms, spend b payload bits selecting among them.
  3. Control. The same lexicon and the same eligibility rule, but random words and random choices at a matched edit rate. This is what isolates the mechanism from the mere act of editing.
  4. Cost. GPT-2 perplexity, a judge independent of the generator, so no arm gets home-field advantage.

Both the watermarked and the unwatermarked corpus go through every attack. Scoring attacked positives against unattacked negatives would let the attack shift both distributions and masquerade as watermark destruction.

The corpus is frozen, not regenerated

Every run scores one fixed corpus on disk. This is not only a speed matter — it is what makes the arms comparable, since regenerating between runs would let sampling noise masquerade as an attack effect.

Positives cannot be reused from any existing dataset. SynthID biases sampling as text is generated, so watermarked text exists only if it was generated under the key; there is no way to stamp a watermark onto prose after the fact. The 56 watermarked texts were generated once and are now reused verbatim.

Negatives have no such constraint. Any text not generated under the key is a valid negative, so these are drawn from the IMDB reviews already in datasets/ rather than generated. Human prose is the more honest negative anyway: there is no question of it secretly carrying a watermark.

Swapping the negatives is only legitimate if unwatermarked text scores at the null regardless of domain, so that was verified rather than assumed. The negatives are also truncated to match the positives' length distribution, because longer texts concentrate more tightly around the null and would otherwise tighten the 1%-FPR threshold in the detector's favour.

Does payload-driven selection actually help?

SynSteg leaves less signal than its control at b=1 and b=2, which invites the claim that choosing synonyms by payload is somehow better at scrubbing the watermark. Both attacks run on the same texts, so the question can be settled with a paired test rather than an eyeball.

The effect is real at b=1 and b=2 and vanishes at b=3. But size matters more than significance here: the largest advantage is 0.007 mean g-value against a baseline lift of 0.080, so payload-driven selection accounts for under a tenth of the removal. The other nine tenths come from the edit rate, which the control matches exactly. An attacker gains essentially nothing from SynSteg's machinery that they could not get by picking words out of a hat — and pays the same tenfold perplexity penalty either way.

The readable attack leaves the watermark intact

Raw WordNet substitution overstates the damage a real attacker would accept, since it ignores context. Restricting substitutions to those a masked language model finds plausible in place gives a fair picture of what removal costs when the text still has to read well.

At every quality-preserving operating point the detector is still perfect — not merely good, but catching every watermarked text at a 1% false positive rate. Even the loosest filter, which already costs 50% more perplexity and rewrites one word in fifteen, leaves two-thirds of the watermark signal. Payload capacity collapses in step: 33 bits per text, against 160 for the unrestricted attack.

The corner that matters is empty

What the aggressive attack does to the text

The perplexity multiplier is abstract; the output is not. Both passages are the opening of the same generated paragraph.

Watermarked, unattacked — perplexity 15.1

Technology has profoundly transformed every aspect of human life, both positively and negatively, shaping societies in profound ways. On one hand, technology offers unprecedented opportunities for communication, commerce, entertainment, and education. It facilitates the exchange of vast amounts of information across borders…

SynSteg b=2 — perplexity 217.8

Technology has profoundly transform every facet of human aliveness, both positively and negatively, shaping companionship in sound agency. On one give, technology extend unprecedented opportunities for communication, commercialism, entertainment, and education. It ease the commutation of brobdingnagian amounts of entropy across border…

This is what a 14× perplexity increase looks like. Any classifier that flags machine-mangled prose — or any human reader — identifies this instantly, so suppressing the watermark here has not bought the attacker anonymity. It has traded one detectable signature for a louder one.

What this does not show

  • This is a proxy, not real Claude output. Anthropic's watermark key and detector are private. The measurement uses an open SynthID-Text instance, which is the same algorithm family by Anthropic's own description, but their parameters, tournament depth, and detector are unknown. Absolute numbers would shift; the shape of the trade-off should not.
  • The generator is small. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct at roughly 185 words per text. Longer texts carry more n-grams and are harder to scrub, so this setting favours the attacker.
  • Four degenerate generations were dropped. The original 60 included texts as short as three words, which carry too few n-grams to score in either direction. Excluding them is why n = 56.
  • The synonym source is WordNet, not GPT-4o. The repository's encoder queries GPT-4o per five-word chunk. WordNet is context-blind, which is exactly why the raw arm reads so badly; the fluency-filtered arm is the closer analogue of GPT-4o quality. Running the real encoder on these containers would tighten the quality estimate and is the natural next step.
  • Only the mean g-value detector was used. The Bayesian detector in the SynthID paper is strictly stronger, so these AUCs are a lower bound on detectability — the real watermark is harder to remove than shown here, not easier.
  • C2PA metadata is out of scope. The file-level provenance signature Anthropic attaches is separate from the text watermark and is stripped by any format change or copy-paste.

Reproducing this

The corpus is built once from texts already on disk; every run after that is pure scoring. A full sweep takes about four minutes on an M-series laptop using the MPS backend.

synthid_lib.py        SynthID generation + mean g-value detector, AUC, TPR@FPR
attacks.py            SynSteg substitution, random control, shared lexicon
fluent_attack.py      masked-LM fluency filter over candidate synonyms
build_corpus.py       freezes positives + IMDB negatives to wm_corpus.jsonl
check_null.py         verifies the negatives sit at the 0.5 null
run_from_corpus.py    all arms, scored from the frozen corpus, no generation
paired_test.py        bootstrap CI on the SynSteg-minus-control difference
summarize_watermark.py  flattens results into the tables above

python build_corpus.py                     # once
python check_null.py                       # validate before trusting any AUC
python run_from_corpus.py --bits 1,2,3 --margins 1.0,3.0,6.0
python paired_test.py
python summarize_watermark.py results/watermark_corpus.json

Companion to the SynSteg vs. Conversation-Steganography benchmark. Detector null for the mean g-value statistic is 0.5000; the IMDB negative corpus measured 0.49757, within 1.5 standard errors of the null, confirming the detector is unbiased on unwatermarked human text.
